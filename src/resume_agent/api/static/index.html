    Writing your career file

    The app never makes anything up. It picks from what you write here and rewords it to suit the job, so how good a resume can be is set by how good this is. Nothing below is about file formats — you fill in forms and the app handles the rest.

    Getting started

    The My information tab has a New button. It asks for your name and gives you empty forms to fill in. You can also start from the worked example, which is useful for seeing what a finished one looks like — but everything in it belongs to a made-up person, so read it rather than edit it.

    What you write stays on this computer and is never uploaded anywhere except when you ask for a resume. Every save keeps a dated backup, What you write is saved to your account, where nobody else who signs in can see it, and goes to the AI provider only when you ask for a resume. Every save is kept, and a save that would break something is refused before anything is written, so you cannot lose work by mistyping.

    An achievement is the unit that matters

    Everything on the finished resume is a rephrasing of one achievement you wrote. They are never split or merged, so write each one whole, in full, and truthfully.

    • What you did — one accomplishment in one or two sentences, as you would say it out loud. Not a job description; a thing that happened.
    • Numbers — recorded as named values. These are the only figures the agent may use when it rewrites this achievement. A number that is not here, and not derivable from here, counts as invented: the rewrite is rejected, retried, and then dropped with a loud note. That is the rule that makes the output trustworthy.
    • Technologies — picked from your Skills list. The picker only offers what you have recorded, which is why this can never be wrong.
    • Themes — your own grouping. At most three achievements per theme reach a page, so if everything is tagged backend most of your best work can never be selected together. Spreading themes is the cheapest way to improve a resume.
    • Confidence — mark anything you could not defend in an interview as a claim, and it is excluded whenever you run with strict.
    • Evidence — where you could prove it. Never printed. It is a note to yourself for the interview.

    It can only name skills you have listed

    If a generated line names a tool that is not in your Skills, the app treats it as made up and throws the line away. That is what stops it quietly turning “a queue” into “Kafka” because the job ad mentioned Kafka.

    Give each skill the other spellings a job ad might use — k8s for Kubernetes, postgres for PostgreSQL. That is how an ad gets matched to your experience, so a missing spelling costs you a match you had actually earned. Removing a skill that achievements already name will be refused; the form warns you first, with a count.

    Narratives are for why, not what

    Plain paragraphs about how you work, what you care about, the hardest thing you have debugged. No formatting to learn — a title and prose. The cover letter draws its voice from here.

    One rule: do not introduce numbers or technologies that appear nowhere else in your career file. The letter is checked the same way the resume is, so a narrative mentioning a framework you never recorded produces letters that fail verification until the agent gives up and writes none at all. Keep them qualitative — the facts live in the achievements.

    Making a resume fit a posting better

    • Write more achievements than fit. Selection is a knapsack over a measured line budget; it can only choose from what exists.
    • Record the numbers. An achievement with none can still be picked, but it cannot be made specific, and specific lines score higher against requirements.
    • Spread the themes, for the three-per-theme reason above.
    • Use the names postings use. Aliases are what retrieval and the keyword pass both read.
    • Run Tailor and read the gaps first. A missing must-have is sometimes an achievement you forgot to write down, not a job you cannot do.

    What the app will not do

    • Print a number that is not in that achievement's recorded numbers.
    • Name a technology that is not in your Skills.
    • Keep anything that fails those checks twice — it is dropped and logged, never quietly kept.
    • Touch the page geometry. It shortens what it writes; it does not shrink your margins to make more fit.

    Where your education goes, and why it moves

    A resume for someone still studying leads with Education, carries the GPA, the honours and the awards, and gives room to leadership and publications — because that is the strongest evidence there is. A resume for someone with a few years of work leads with Experience, and education shrinks to one line at the bottom.

    You do not choose between those. The app counts: months of non-internship work, and whether your studies have finished. Under two years, or still studying, and you get the first layout; past that, the second. An internship is experience and prints as experience — it just does not, on its own, move education off the top.

    The layout control on the Create Resume tab overrides it (turn on Advanced to see it). Worth using if you are changing career, or coming back from a research degree — cases where the months are real but in another field, and counting gives a confidently wrong answer.

    What the layout never decides is what appears. A section shows up when its achievements win space against everything else competing for the page; if none of them do, the section is simply not there. That is why an experienced profile's leadership section quietly disappears rather than being switched off.

    Two pages, and the summary

    Pages defaults to one, which is what most readers expect and what the selection is tuned for. Two is there for the profiles that genuinely need it — an academic record, or a new graduate whose coursework and leadership are the evidence. The capacity of each is measured by compiling, not estimated, so a two-page run really does get to use the second page rather than spilling onto it.

    Summary writes the opening paragraph, and it is off by default: it costs about four lines of a roughly thirty-line page, and those lines are only worth spending when it says something the achievements underneath do not. It is written from the achievements that were selected for that resume and checked against them — a number or a technology that none of them supports gets the summary rewritten, and if that fails twice the resume ships without one.

    Removing things

    Open a job, project, leadership role, publication or narrative and use Remove in the editor bar. It says how many achievements go with the file before it does anything, and keeps a timestamped copy in the backups folder either way.

    Your name and your Skills table have no Remove button: the profile will not load without them. To empty them, clear the fields instead. A whole section you want gone — all your projects, say — is removed one file at a time, or asked for in Chat.

    Talking to it instead of typing

    The Chat tab does two things, and picks between them by reading your message. Ask a question and it answers from a count of what is actually in your profile — which achievements record no numbers, which themes are over the three-per-page cap, which skills nothing uses. It is reading, not guessing.

    Or just tell it what you did, in whatever order it comes out, and it turns that into achievements against the right role. Nothing it proposes is saved until you press the button. Anything it wrote that you did not say — a number, a technology — arrives unticked and flagged in red, because your message is the only thing it is allowed to draw from. Asking to remove something works the same way: it shows you what would go, and waits.

    It cannot change anything on its own. If it ever replies that it has edited or removed something without showing you a card to confirm, nothing happened.

    If you would rather edit the files

    Everything above is stored as YAML and Markdown in profile/, and the Raw file switch in the editor shows the file behind the form. You never need it — but a file edited by hand keeps its comments and formatting, and the forms leave them alone too.
